The Contractor Sales Process: A Step-by-Step Framework

A contractor sales process is a defined, repeatable sequence of steps that a contracting company follows to move a prospect from initial contact to signed contract and successful project handoff. It replaces the informal approach that most contractors use, where each salesperson sells differently based on personal style and instinct, with a structured system that the entire team operates within and leadership can measure, coach, and improve over time.

Most contracting companies do not have a written sales process. Sales Transformation Group has assessed over 1,000 contractors since 2017, spanning roofing, HVAC, plumbing, electrical, and general contracting, and the pattern is remarkably consistent: companies without a defined process have unpredictable revenue, inconsistent close rates, high rep turnover, and no ability to diagnose where deals are falling apart. The owner knows the company should be closing more, but cannot identify why it is not because there is no process to audit.

This page presents a step-by-step contractor sales process framework based on the Baseline Selling methodology that STG has implemented with over 1,000 contractors. It covers each stage of the process, the common mistakes that contractors make, and the different paths to implementation depending on your company's size and maturity.

Why Contractors Need a Defined Sales Process

The contracting industry has operated without formal sales processes for decades. Owners built their companies on personal relationships, word-of-mouth referrals, and the ability to outwork competitors. That approach works at small scale but becomes a liability as companies grow. The problems that a missing sales process creates compound quietly until they become impossible to ignore.

Inconsistent Performance Across Reps

Without a defined process, every salesperson on your team sells differently. One rep qualifies thoroughly. Another skips straight to the proposal. One follows up religiously. Another lets leads go cold after one attempt. The result is wildly inconsistent performance that leadership cannot coach because there is no standard to coach against. When STG conducts competency assessments with contracting companies, the gap between the best and worst rep on the same team is often dramatic, not because of talent differences but because of process differences. A defined sales process establishes the minimum standard that every rep must follow, which raises the floor of performance across the entire team.

Revenue Ceilings That Marketing Cannot Fix

Contracting companies that plateau at $3 million, $5 million, or $10 million typically assume they need more leads. STG's data from assessing over 1,000 contractors tells a different story. Most companies have enough lead volume. The problem is conversion. Deals stall because reps do not qualify properly, present before building value, or fail to follow up on open proposals. Kingdom Roofing was a $4 million company generating adequate leads. Within three years of implementing a structured sales process with STG, they grew to $16 million. The leads were already there. The process to convert them was not.

Vulnerability to Rep Turnover

When your sales process lives in your top rep's head, it leaves when they leave. Companies without a defined process lose institutional knowledge every time a salesperson departs, and rebuilding that knowledge with a new hire takes months. A documented process means that new reps can be onboarded against a clear standard, trained on specific steps, and productive faster because they are not reinventing the wheel. Rackley Roofing grew their team from 3 to 8 salespeople over three and a half years while improving close ratios because the process was the system, not any individual person. New reps joined a defined structure and were coached against clear milestones rather than being told to shadow the senior rep for two weeks and figure it out.

The Baseline Selling Framework for Contractors

Sales Transformation Group's contractor sales process is built on the Baseline Selling methodology, a consultative, value-based approach that uses a baseball analogy to make the sales process intuitive and memorable. Ryan Groth, STG's founder and a former professional baseball player, adapted Baseline Selling specifically for the contracting industry after working with over 1,000 companies across the trades.

The baseball framework works because it gives reps and managers a shared language for discussing where a deal stands and what needs to happen next. Instead of vague pipeline stages like "proposal sent" or "follow-up needed," the team speaks in terms of bases: Is this prospect on first? Have we advanced to second? What do we need to get to third? This shared language eliminates confusion and makes coaching conversations specific and actionable.

Damschroder Roofing credited the baseball analogy as the turning point in their sales approach. After years stuck at $5 million, the team adopted Baseline Selling and grew to $10.1 million by 2021. The framework gave their reps a visual, memorable process that replaced the improvised selling they had been doing for years.

The Contractor Sales Process: Step by Step

The following framework presents a nine-step contractor sales process adapted from the Baseline Selling methodology. Each step includes what happens, why it matters, and the qualifying criteria that must be met before advancing to the next stage. This process applies to both residential and commercial contractors, though the execution details differ by segment.

Step 1: Lead Intake and Initial Response

Every sale begins with a lead, whether from a phone call, web form, referral, door knock, or commercial prospecting effort. The first step in the process is capturing the lead with complete information and responding within a defined timeframe. Most contracting companies lose deals before they start because leads sit unanswered for hours or days. The standard should be a response within minutes for inbound leads, with a documented intake process that captures the prospect's name, contact information, project type, location, and how they heard about the company. STG's assessments of over 1,000 contractors show that companies with a defined intake process and response time standard close at higher rates than those that leave lead response to individual rep initiative. This step seems simple, but it is where the process begins and where many contractors first fall behind.

Step 2: Pre-Qualification (Getting to First Base)

Before investing time in a full appointment, the rep must determine whether this prospect is worth pursuing. Pre-qualification is a brief conversation, typically five to ten minutes by phone, that confirms four things: the prospect has a genuine need, they have the authority to make or influence the decision, there is a realistic budget or willingness to invest, and the timeline aligns with your capacity. In Baseline Selling terms, this is getting to first base. You cannot advance a deal if the prospect does not meet these basic criteria. STG's data consistently shows that poor pre-qualification is the single largest source of wasted sales capacity in contracting companies. Reps drive across town for appointments with people who were never going to buy, wasting two to three hours per unqualified appointment. A five-minute phone call eliminates the majority of these wasted trips.

Step 3: Discovery and Needs Assessment (Advancing to Second Base)

Once a prospect is pre-qualified, the next step is a thorough discovery conversation that uncovers the full scope of their need, their decision-making process, their concerns, and what a successful outcome looks like to them. This is not a product pitch. It is a consultative conversation where the rep asks questions and listens. In Baseline Selling, this is advancing to second base by understanding the prospect's pain. For residential contractors, discovery happens during the in-home or on-site appointment. The rep inspects the roof, assesses the HVAC system, or evaluates the plumbing issue while asking questions about the homeowner's priorities, budget expectations, and timeline. For commercial contractors, discovery may happen across multiple meetings with different stakeholders. The goal at this stage is to understand the prospect well enough to present a solution that addresses their specific situation rather than delivering a generic pitch.

Step 4: Value-Building Presentation

With discovery complete, the rep delivers a structured presentation that connects the company's solution to the prospect's specific needs. This is not reading a brochure or reciting features. A value-building presentation uses the information gathered during discovery to explain why this solution is the right fit for this particular prospect. For a roofing company, this might include showing inspection photos, explaining material options with pros and cons specific to the homeowner's climate and roof structure, walking through warranty differences, and addressing the specific concerns the homeowner raised during discovery. The presentation builds value before price ever enters the conversation. STG's Baseline Selling approach teaches reps to invest the majority of the sales conversation in building value because when the prospect understands the full value of the solution, price resistance decreases naturally.

Step 5: Proposal and Investment Discussion

After the value has been established, the rep presents the proposal with specific pricing. In a well-run contractor sales process, the price should feel like a natural extension of the value conversation, not a jarring surprise. The proposal should clearly itemize what is included, explain the scope, and present payment options or financing if applicable. This step is where many contractors lose deals because they present the price without sufficient value-building, or they email a PDF proposal and hope for a callback. The Baseline Selling framework teaches reps to present the investment in person whenever possible and to tie the price directly to the value established in the previous step. Ace Roofing's 50 percent growth in three consecutive years was driven in part by their transition from emailing proposals to presenting them live within a structured conversation, a change that came directly from implementing the Baseline Selling process.

Step 6: Objection Resolution (Reaching Third Base)

Objections are a natural part of every contractor sales process. The most common include "I need more quotes," "Your price is too high," "I need to think about it," and "I need to talk to my spouse or partner." In Baseline Selling, reaching third base means resolving these objections through value-based questioning rather than high-pressure tactics. The rep's job is to uncover the real concern behind the stated objection. A homeowner who says "Your price is too high" may actually be uncertain about the value, worried about financing, or comparing against a low-bid competitor who is cutting scope. A trained rep asks questions to surface the real barrier and addresses it directly. STG's training programs include specific objection-handling frameworks for the most common contractor scenarios. After working with over 1,000 contractors, these frameworks have been refined to address the exact objections that roofing, HVAC, plumbing, and electrical salespeople encounter daily.

Step 7: Close and Contract (Scoring the Run)

When a prospect has been qualified, understood, presented with value, and had their objections resolved, the close is the logical next step, not a high-pressure moment. In Baseline Selling, this is scoring the run. The rep confirms the agreement, walks through the contract, sets expectations for the project timeline, and secures the commitment. The close should include a clear next step: when the project starts, who the customer's point of contact will be during the work, and what happens between now and the start date. Companies with a defined closing step in their process convert at higher rates because nothing is left ambiguous. The customer knows what they agreed to, what happens next, and who to contact with questions. This clarity reduces cancellations and builds trust before the project even begins.

Step 8: Production Handoff

The sale does not end at the signed contract. A critical step that many contractors skip is the formal handoff from sales to production. This step ensures that everything the salesperson promised is communicated accurately to the project manager and crew. Scope, special conditions, customer preferences, and timeline commitments must transfer cleanly. When this handoff is informal or nonexistent, the production team shows up without full context, the customer feels unheard, and the salesperson gets pulled back into managing the job instead of selling the next one. STG's 36-milestone journey includes production handoff protocols because, based on over 1,000 contractor assessments, failed handoffs are one of the top sources of customer dissatisfaction, callback costs, and rep distraction. A five-minute structured handoff meeting or documented form prevents hours of problems downstream.

Step 9: Post-Project Follow-Up and Referral Generation

The final step in the contractor sales process is following up after the project is complete to confirm satisfaction, request a review, and ask for referrals. Most contractors skip this step entirely, which leaves significant revenue on the table. A satisfied customer is the highest-quality lead source available because their referral comes with built-in trust. The follow-up should happen within one to two weeks of project completion and include a brief quality check, a request for a Google review, and a specific referral ask. STG's clients maintain a 5.0-star rating across 500-plus Google reviews in part because structured post-project follow-up is built into the process, not left to chance. Every completed project should feed the top of the pipeline for the next project through intentional referral generation.

Common Mistakes Contractors Make in Their Sales Process

Sales Transformation Group's assessment of over 1,000 contractors has revealed consistent patterns of mistakes that limit growth. These are not failures of effort or talent. They are structural problems that exist because no one built the process to prevent them.

Mistake 1: Skipping Qualification

The most prevalent mistake is jumping from lead intake directly to the proposal without qualifying the prospect. Reps treat every lead as equal and invest the same time in a homeowner who is casually shopping as they do in one who is ready to buy. The result is hours spent on appointments and proposals that never had a realistic chance of closing. Rackley Roofing dramatically improved their close ratios after implementing a qualification framework because their reps stopped spending time on unqualified prospects. This single change had a greater impact on revenue than any closing technique because it concentrated selling time on the opportunities most likely to convert.

Mistake 2: Presenting Price Before Value

Contractors often rush to the proposal because they believe the customer just wants a number. This creates a race to the bottom where the lowest price wins and margins shrink. The contractor sales process outlined above deliberately places the value-building presentation before the investment discussion because the order matters. When a prospect understands the full value of your materials, workmanship, warranty, and company reputation, they evaluate your price against that value. When they see the price first, they evaluate it against the cheapest competitor. STG's Baseline Selling methodology explicitly addresses this sequencing because it is the most common structural flaw in contractor sales conversations.

Mistake 3: No Follow-Up System

After assessing over 1,000 contractors, STG has found that most companies have no systematic follow-up process. A rep presents a proposal, the customer says they need to think about it, and the rep moves on to the next lead without a defined follow-up cadence. Industry data suggests that the majority of sales happen after multiple follow-up contacts, yet most reps give up after one or two attempts. A contractor sales process must include specific follow-up steps with defined timing: a call 48 hours after the proposal, a second touch at one week, a third at two weeks. Without this structure, companies lose deals they should have won simply because they stopped reaching out before the customer was ready to decide.

Mistake 4: No Sales Meetings or Coaching

Many contracting companies either hold no regular sales meetings or run meetings that are status updates with no coaching component. A weekly sales meeting should be a structured coaching session where the team reviews pipeline, practices skills, and holds each other accountable to the process. STG's fractional sales leaders run these meetings as part of the Platinum program, and the meeting structure has been refined across over 1,000 contractor engagements. Companies like National Roofing Partners credited these structured meetings, facilitated by STG coach Victoria, as instrumental in exceeding their profit targets.

Mistake 5: Relying on the Owner as the Sales Process

When the owner is the best salesperson and the only person who can close large deals, the company has a person, not a process. This limits growth to the owner's personal capacity and creates a single point of failure. Greenwood Industries engaged STG specifically to build a sales process that did not depend on any individual. Their service division grew from $3 million to $13 million because the process became the system that multiple reps could execute, not a set of skills that lived in one person's head.

Mistake 6: No Production Handoff Protocol

The gap between what the salesperson promises and what the production team delivers is where customer satisfaction dies. When there is no formal handoff process, details get lost, expectations go unmet, and the salesperson gets pulled back into project management. This is not a production problem. It is a sales process problem because the handoff is the final step of the sale. STG includes handoff protocols in the 36-milestone journey because, after 1,000-plus assessments, the data is clear: companies with a defined handoff have fewer callbacks, higher customer satisfaction scores, and more referrals.

Mistake 7: Treating Sales as Individual Performance, Not a System

The most fundamental mistake is the belief that sales success depends on hiring the right people rather than building the right process. Talented salespeople are valuable, but they cannot compensate for a missing system. When a top rep leaves a company with no process, revenue drops immediately. When a top rep leaves a company with a strong process, the system absorbs the loss and the replacement gets productive faster. Damschroder Roofing was stuck at $5 million for years despite having capable people. The breakthrough came when they stopped relying on individual effort and started implementing STG's systematic framework. The process, not a single person, drove the growth to $10.1 million.

How to Implement a Contractor Sales Process

Knowing the steps of a contractor sales process is different from implementing one. Implementation requires choosing the right approach for your company's current stage and committing to the consistent reinforcement that turns a written process into actual team behavior.

Option 1: DIY Implementation

Owners who prefer to build the process themselves can start by documenting their current sales flow, identifying the gaps against the nine-step framework above, and introducing changes one step at a time. The advantage of DIY is cost. The disadvantage is that most owners are too close to their own process to evaluate it objectively, and they lack the time to consistently coach and reinforce new behaviors while running the rest of the business. STG's assessment data from over 1,000 contractors shows that self-directed process improvements have the lowest adoption rate because there is no external accountability to sustain the change. DIY works best for owners who are disciplined self-learners and have the time to dedicate to the sales management function alongside their other responsibilities.

Frequently Asked Questions About the Contractor Sales Process

What is a contractor sales process?

A contractor sales process is a defined, repeatable sequence of steps that a contracting company follows to move a prospect from initial contact to signed contract. It includes lead qualification, needs assessment, presentation, proposal, objection handling, closing, and post-sale handoff. Unlike informal selling where each rep does it differently, a defined sales process creates consistency across the entire team and gives leadership the ability to measure, coach, and improve performance at every stage. Sales Transformation Group has assessed over 1,000 contractors since 2017, and the most consistent finding is that companies without a defined process have unpredictable revenue, inconsistent close rates, and no ability to diagnose where deals are falling apart. A process turns sales from an individual talent exercise into an organizational system.

How many steps should a contractor sales process have?

An effective contractor sales process typically has seven to ten defined steps, depending on the complexity of the work and whether the company serves residential or commercial customers. Residential contractors can often operate with seven steps because the sales cycle is shorter and involves fewer decision-makers. Commercial contractors may need nine or ten steps to account for multi-stakeholder approvals, formal bid requirements, and longer evaluation periods. Sales Transformation Group's framework based on the Baseline Selling methodology uses a structured progression that maps to the baseball analogy: getting on base through qualification, advancing through value-building and commitment, and scoring through closing and handoff. The exact number of steps matters less than having each step clearly defined with specific actions, qualifying criteria, and exit conditions so reps and managers know exactly what needs to happen before advancing to the next stage.

What is the biggest mistake contractors make in their sales process?

The biggest mistake contractors make is skipping the qualification step and jumping straight to the proposal. Sales Transformation Group's assessments of over 1,000 contractors consistently reveal that poor qualification is the single largest source of wasted sales capacity. Reps spend hours preparing proposals for prospects who were never going to buy, who do not have budget authority, or who are simply collecting quotes to negotiate down their preferred contractor. A well-designed sales process requires reps to confirm budget, authority, need, and timeline before investing time in a full presentation or proposal. Rackley Roofing improved their close ratios dramatically after implementing STG's qualification framework. Their team grew from 3 to 8 salespeople during the same period, which means they were closing at higher rates even with less experienced reps, because the process filtered out unqualified prospects before they consumed selling time.
